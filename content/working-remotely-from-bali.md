Two separate questions get mixed together here. Can you physically work from Bali, and can you legally work from Bali. The answers are different.

## The infrastructure is fine now

**Fibre connections of 50 to 100 Mbps are standard** in the main expat areas. That is a genuine change from a few years ago, when the answer to this question was much less encouraging.

For calls, screen sharing and normal remote work, it is not a limitation.

What still catches people:

- **Power cuts happen.** Usually short. A laptop with battery is fine, a desktop is not.
- **Villa internet varies wildly.** The villa's connection is not the area's connection. Test it before you sign anything longer than a month.
- **Get a local SIM immediately** with a decent data package. It is cheap and it is your backup when the villa connection drops mid-call.
- **Time zones.** Excellent for Asia and Australia. Workable for Europe with early starts. Genuinely hard for the US.

## The legal position

This is where it matters, and it changed this year.

In **April 2026** Indonesian immigration launched a dedicated Bali patrol task force. In its first three weeks it **detained 62 foreign nationals** for illegal work, overstays and document violations. Officers patrol Canggu, Ubud, Seminyak, Kerobokan and Uluwatu, and they check public social media and LinkedIn.

Working on a tourist visa is not a grey area and is being enforced. [The detail is here](/visas/bali-visa-crackdown-2026/).

## The permit that covers it

**E33G** exists specifically for people employed by or contracted to a company outside Indonesia. The published criteria are specific:

- A genuine foreign employment or consulting agreement
- Minimum annual income commonly cited at **USD 60,000**
- No income from Indonesian entities at all

It runs a year and renews while both conditions hold. [Full requirements here](/visas/digital-nomad-bali/).

## The thing nobody plans for

**Tax residence follows physical presence, not your visa.** Cross 183 days in a year and you are an Indonesian tax resident, even though every dollar you earn is foreign and paid abroad.

This is the most commonly misunderstood point among remote workers here, and it is worth a conversation with a tax adviser before you cross that line rather than after.

## Where to actually work

Canggu has the highest concentration of cafés and coworking, and the highest concentration of people doing the same thing as you. Ubud is quieter and cheaper. Sanur is the underrated one for anyone who wants to work rather than network.

## The honest summary

The connectivity question is basically solved. The legal question is not, and it got significantly more serious in April.

If you are here for months rather than weeks, get the right permit. It costs less than a deportation and a multi-year ban.

## The infrastructure, honestly

| | Position |
| --- | --- |
| Fibre internet | 50 to 100 Mbps standard in the main expat areas |
| Mobile data | Good coverage, cheap, a reliable backup |
| Power | Mostly stable, with short outages; a laptop battery covers most |
| Coworking | Extensive in Canggu, good in Ubud and Sanur |
| Cafes | Abundant, though wifi quality varies widely |

For calls, screen sharing and normal knowledge work, this is sufficient. For anything requiring guaranteed uptime, run a mobile data backup and do not rely on a single connection.

## Time zones are the real constraint

Bali is UTC+8. That works well with Asia and Australia, awkwardly with Europe, and badly with North America.

| Your team | Reality |
| --- | --- |
| Australia and Asia | Comfortable overlap |
| Europe | Afternoons and evenings |
| US East Coast | Late nights |
| US West Coast | Very late nights, or very early mornings |

People underestimate how much a permanent 3am call schedule erodes the reason they came. If your team is American, work out the actual meeting times before you commit, not after.

## The permit question

Working remotely on a visitor visa is a violation and is being actively enforced. Since 15 April 2026, roughly 100 officers have been deployed across Canggu, Ubud, Seminyak, Kerobokan and Uluwatu, with 62 foreign nationals detained in the first three weeks.

The E33G is the lawful route for people employed by or contracted to a company established outside Indonesia. It requires a documented foreign employment relationship, a minimum annual income commonly cited at USD 60,000, and no income from Indonesian entities.

Officers monitor public social media and LinkedIn profiles. Stating publicly that you are based in Bali while working, on a visitor visa, is evidence.

## Where to base yourself

**Canggu** has the highest density of coworking, cafes and other remote workers, and the worst traffic on the island.

**Ubud** is quieter and cheaper, with a wellness-oriented community and a slower pace.

**Sanur** suits people who want to work rather than network, with hospitals close and light traffic.

## Tax

Presence beyond 183 days in a twelve-month period generally makes you an Indonesian tax resident, regardless of where your employer sits or where you are paid. The permit and the tax position are separate systems.

## Common questions

### Is the internet good enough to work from Bali?
Yes. Fibre connections of 50 to 100 Mbps are standard in the main expat areas, which is sufficient for calls, screen sharing and normal remote work.

### What are the best areas in Bali for remote work?
Canggu has the highest concentration of cafes and coworking spaces, Ubud is quieter and cheaper, and Sanur suits people who want to work rather than network.

### Do you need a visa to work remotely from Bali?
Yes. Working on a tourist visa is a violation and is actively enforced. The E33G permit is built for remote workers employed by foreign companies.

### Is the power reliable in Bali?
Mostly, with occasional short outages. A laptop with battery and a local SIM with a decent data package covers you when the villa connection drops.
