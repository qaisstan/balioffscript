This is the part of Bali that the marketing does not cover, and it increasingly affects owners commercially rather than only ethically.

## Water

Bali's water demand has grown far faster than its supply. Tourism is water-intensive: pools, laundry, showers, golf, landscaping. Groundwater extraction has increased accordingly, and in parts of the south the water table has fallen.

For a property owner this shows up three ways:

**Operating cost.** Where piped supply is absent or insufficient, trucked water is a real recurring expense that rises in dry season.

**Regulatory risk.** Groundwater extraction is regulated and scrutiny has been increasing. A villa whose entire supply depends on an unpermitted well is carrying a risk that has been getting larger, not smaller.

**Long-term viability.** A development model built on unlimited cheap groundwater is not a safe assumption for a twenty-five year lease.

The Bukit is the sharpest example: limestone geology, little accessible groundwater, and many properties reliant on trucked supply.

## Waste

Bali's waste infrastructure has not kept pace with either population or tourism. Collection is inconsistent outside the main areas, landfill capacity is strained, and a significant share of waste is burned or ends up in rivers.

In wet season the rivers carry it to the beaches, which is why images of rubbish on Kuta beach circulate every year. It is seasonal, it is real, and it affects the product you are selling to guests.

## What owners can actually do

- Arrange proper waste collection rather than assuming it happens
- Separate and use the private recycling operations that exist
- Avoid burning, which is common and genuinely harmful
- Install water-efficient fittings and a pool cover, which cuts evaporation substantially
- Harvest rainwater, which is straightforward here given the wet season volume
- Ensure any well is properly permitted
- Treat wastewater properly rather than to the minimum

## Why this is commercial

Guests notice. Reviews mention it. Areas that degrade lose the demand that made them valuable.

More concretely, the regulatory direction on water in particular is one way. Assume extraction rules tighten across the life of your investment, and be sceptical of any project whose economics depend on them not doing so.

## The honest framing

None of this makes Bali a bad place to own property. It makes it a place with finite resources under real pressure, where the sensible position is to build and operate as though the constraints are permanent, because they are.

## Common questions

### Is Bali running out of water?
Parts of the south face genuine groundwater stress, driven by tourism demand growing faster than supply. It is an operating cost issue and an increasing regulatory one.

### Do I need a permit for a well in Bali?
Yes. Groundwater extraction is regulated, and scrutiny has been increasing. An unpermitted well is a growing risk.

### Why is there rubbish on Bali beaches?
Waste infrastructure has not kept pace with growth. In wet season rivers carry accumulated waste to the coast, which is why it appears seasonally.

### Does the environment affect property values in Bali?
Indirectly but really. Areas that degrade lose guest demand, and tightening water regulation affects what a property can lawfully operate on.

### What can a villa owner do about water use?
Efficient fittings, a pool cover to cut evaporation, rainwater harvesting, a properly permitted supply, and proper wastewater treatment.
