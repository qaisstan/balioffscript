Bali's environmental pressures are real, they affect property values and rental income directly, and they are moving in one direction.

## Water

The island's water situation is under genuine strain from tourism, agriculture and development competing for a finite supply.

**Groundwater extraction is regulated and requires a permit**, and enforcement has increased as depletion has become a recognised issue. A property drawing from an unpermitted bore is carrying a compliance risk that grows rather than shrinks.

**The Bukit is the acute case.** Limestone geology means deep groundwater, expensive and uncertain boreholes and inconsistent mains supply. A large number of Bukit villas run on **trucked water**, which is a real recurring cost and a genuine operational risk when every villa on the peninsula needs water simultaneously in August.

**The traditional subak irrigation system** — the cooperative water management that shapes Bali's rice terraces and is UNESCO recognised — depends on water that development upstream increasingly diverts. That is a cultural and agricultural issue and also a practical one, because it affects the landscape that the tourism economy is selling.

For a buyer, the practical question is specific: **where does this property's water come from, what does it cost annually, is the extraction permitted, and what happens in peak season?** Get it in writing.

## Waste

Bali's waste infrastructure has not kept pace with visitor numbers and consumption.

Landfill capacity is constrained, collection is inconsistent outside the main areas, and the wet season brings visible accumulation on beaches as rivers carry waste to the coast. This is worst between roughly December and March and it appears in guest reviews.

There are genuine initiatives — beach cleanups, waste banks, plastic reduction regulation — and they have had real effects. The scale of the problem remains larger than the response.

For an owner, two consequences. **Guest experience** is affected in the wet season, particularly on west coast beaches. And **waste management at the property** — separation, collection arrangements, composting — is something a well-run villa handles and a badly run one does not, and guests notice.

## Drainage and flooding

Rapid development has covered ground that previously absorbed rainfall, and drainage infrastructure was built for a different volume.

Several areas flood reliably in heavy rain, and it is **street-specific** rather than area-general. Umalas, parts of Kerobokan, parts of Denpasar and low-lying pockets across the south all have a history.

**Ask about the specific street**, not the area, and ideally ask a neighbour rather than the seller.

The **sempadan sungai** — the river setback — exists partly for this reason and is strictly applied, which catches more Canggu and Pererenan parcels than buyers expect.

## Coastal erosion

Several stretches of the south and west coast have lost land. Candidasa's beach loss decades ago is why that area never recovered its earlier position, and breakwaters are in place there now.

For a coastal parcel, **ask what the boundary was and what it is**. A survey from five years ago may not describe the land you are buying.

## Why this affects your investment

**Directly, through operating costs.** Trucked water, drainage maintenance, waste handling.

**Through guest experience**, which drives reviews, which drive ranking and rate.

**Through regulation**, which is tightening. Groundwater permits are enforced more than they were, and the general direction across Bali — nominee arrangements, accommodation licensing, tax administration — is toward more enforcement rather than less.

**Through the underlying product.** Bali sells landscape, water and beaches. Degradation of those is degradation of the thing the entire property market rests on, and that is a genuine long-term consideration rather than a rhetorical one.

## What a responsible owner actually does

Permit the bore. It is a legal requirement and the enforcement direction is one way.

Install adequate storage, so you buy water less often and are not exposed in peak season.

Fit water-efficient fittings and a pool cover, which reduces evaporation meaningfully in this climate.

Handle waste properly — separation, reliable collection, composting where practical.

Check drainage on the property and fix it, because failure here damages the building as well as the environment.

Design for the climate if you are building: cross-ventilation and shading reduce the aircon load, which is the largest single environmental and financial cost of running a villa here.

None of this is expensive and most of it reduces operating costs. It also happens to be what the guest segment that fills your quiet months increasingly asks about.
