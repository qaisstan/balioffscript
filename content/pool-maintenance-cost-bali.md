In a Bali villa the pool is not an amenity, it is the product. It is the cover image, it is why the booking happened, and it is the thing guests photograph. It is also the item most likely to be visibly wrong.

## What it costs to run

**A pool service** — two to three visits a week, chemicals, brushing, skimming, filter cleaning — is an ordinary monthly cost and modest relative to the rest of the operation. Most villas contract it rather than employing someone directly.

**Electricity for the pump** is the larger ongoing number and it is frequently overlooked. A pump running an unnecessarily long daily cycle is a meaningful share of a villa's power bill.

**Water**, for topping up. Evaporation in this climate is substantial, and on the Bukit where water is trucked, pool top-up is a genuine cost that scales with sun and occupancy.

**Chemicals**, continuously, because the tropical combination of heat, sun, rain and bathers is hard on water balance.

**Periodic resurfacing**, which is the capital item. Pool surfaces have a finite life and resurfacing is a several-thousand-dollar job that takes the pool — and effectively the villa — out of service for a period.

## Why it is harder here

**Heat and sun** accelerate chlorine loss, which means more chemical and tighter management.

**Rain** dilutes and unbalances the water, and a heavy wet season downpour changes the chemistry in an afternoon.

**Organic load.** Leaves, frangipani flowers, insects and whatever the garden drops. A pool sited under heavy planting is a permanent cleaning problem, which is why what you plant near it matters.

**Occupancy spikes.** Ten people in a pool for a week is a very different load from two.

**Algae** establishes fast in warm water and a pool that goes wrong on a Friday looks green by Sunday.

## The failures that cost you

**Green water on arrival.** The single worst guest experience a Bali villa can deliver, because the pool is the reason they booked. It produces a refund conversation and a review that persists.

**A pump failure in peak season**, with no spare and a two-day wait for a part.

**Losing water overnight**, which means a leak in the structure or the plumbing and is expensive to find and fix.

**Deferred resurfacing**, where a tired surface becomes rough, stains, and eventually fails properly.

**Over-chlorination**, which irritates guests and is what an inexperienced service does when it has let the balance slip.

## What to check when buying

**Run the pump** and listen to it. Check the skimmers, returns and lighting all work.

**Check the water level holds overnight.** Mark it and come back.

**Look at the surface** — cracks, staining, rough patches, failing grout on tiled pools.

**Ask when it was last resurfaced**, and treat an unknown answer as meaning it is due.

**Ask who services it and how often**, and whether there is a maintenance record.

**Check the equipment housing** for corrosion and improvised repairs.

A pool needing resurfacing is a legitimate and quantifiable reason to reduce an offer. Get a quote and put it on the table.

## Reducing the cost

**Shorter, well-scheduled pump cycles** rather than continuous running. This is the largest saving available and it also aligns with solar generation if you have it.

**A pool cover** where practical, which cuts evaporation meaningfully and reduces chemical use. Less common in Bali than it should be.

**Plant sensibly.** Avoid species that drop heavily into the water. This decision is made at landscaping and pays every week thereafter.

**Good filtration**, specified properly at build. Undersized filtration is a permanent operating cost.

**Fix leaks immediately.** A slow leak is water, chemicals and eventually structural damage.

## The operational discipline

**Daily attention in peak season**, not three times a week. A full villa loads the pool heavily and the margin for error shrinks.

**Brief your manager** to report early, because green water is a process that starts days before it is visible.

**Keep spares** — a pump seal, a spare pump if the property is remote — because a two-day outage with guests in residence costs more than the part.

**Photograph the pool regularly** as part of your own property checks. It is the single best indicator of whether the property is being looked after.

## Common questions

### How much does a Bali villa pool cost to maintain?
A service contract of two to three visits a week is modest monthly, with pump electricity the larger ongoing cost, plus chemicals, top-up water and periodic resurfacing as a capital item.

### Why do Bali pools turn green?
Heat and sun accelerate chlorine loss, rain unbalances the water and organic load from surrounding planting is heavy. Warm water lets algae establish in days.

### How often should a Bali villa pool be serviced?
Two to three times a week as a minimum, and daily attention in peak season when a full villa loads the pool heavily and the margin for error shrinks.

### How do I reduce pool running costs?
Shorter well-scheduled pump cycles rather than continuous running, a pool cover where practical, sensible planting that does not drop into the water, and properly sized filtration.

### What should I check about a pool before buying a villa?
Whether the water level holds overnight, the surface condition, when it was last resurfaced, who services it and how often, and the state of the pump and equipment housing.
