People plan the visa, the house and the business, and then find that the hardest part of living in Bali is the part they never thought about.

## The transience

The foreign community in Bali turns over constantly.

People arrive with a plan, stay two years and leave. Someone you become close to takes a job in Singapore, or has a child and moves home, or simply runs out of reasons to stay.

**It happens repeatedly and it is wearing.** Long-term residents describe it as the single hardest part of living here, and new arrivals almost never anticipate it.

Two practical responses: invest in the people who are actually staying, and build relationships that are not only with other foreigners.

## Where foreigners meet each other

**Around a thing rather than around being foreign.** Surfing, a gym, a sport, music, a class, a co-working space, a school gate, volunteering.

Communities organised around an activity are more durable than ones organised around nationality, and they produce better friendships.

**Canggu and Pererenan** have the densest and youngest foreign population, heavily entrepreneurial and creative, with coworking spaces as the social infrastructure.

**Ubud** skews toward wellness, creative work and longer-established residents.

**Sanur and Umalas** have more families and more people who have been here a long time.

**The Bukit** has a strong surf community with high repeat visitation and a settled core.

Where you live shapes who you meet more than anything else you do.

## The Indonesian community, which matters more

This is the part that separates people who live here from people who are staying here.

**Learn Bahasa Indonesia.** It is unusually learnable — no verb conjugation, no gender, no tones, phonetic spelling — and a few months of genuine effort produces functional conversation. It changes how you are treated, what people tell you, and what you understand about where you are.

**Know your neighbours.** Not as politeness. In Bali, the people around you are the ones who notice when something is wrong at your property, who tell you what is coming, and who help resolve things when they go wrong.

**Engage with the banjar.** The customary neighbourhood association has real authority over local matters — ceremonies, community obligations, boundary and access disputes, land use custom.

Introduce yourself when you arrive. Contribute when asked, which is normal rather than an imposition. Attend when invited. Ask what the local rules are rather than assuming there are none.

**A foreigner with a good relationship with their banjar is in a materially stronger position than one without**, and it is the cheapest investment available to a property owner here. It is also built before there is a problem, not after.

## Respect the ceremonies

**Nyepi** stops the island. **Galungan and Kuningan** are the major recurring celebrations. **Odalan** temple anniversaries happen constantly on a 210-day cycle.

Staff have genuine obligations around them, construction pauses, and roads close.

Planning around them rather than resenting them is both correct and practical, and it is noticed.

## For a property owner specifically

The commercial case is direct.

A villa in a street where the neighbours know who you are, where you have introduced yourself to the banjar, and where staff are part of the local community, is a property people look out for. It gets cooperation on permits, early warning about problems, and help resolving disputes.

A villa owned by an absent foreigner nobody has met is not, and the difference appears exactly when you need something.

If you host events, this relationship is the whole difference between operating indefinitely and operating against local objection, which no permit protects you from.

## The honest difficulties

**Depth takes time.** Friendships built in a transient community move fast and end fast.

**Alcohol is everywhere** in the foreign social scene, particularly in Canggu, and for some people that is a genuine problem worth naming.

**Isolation is real** for people who arrive without a structure — no job to go to, no school gate, no team. Working remotely from a villa can be lonely in a way a holiday never is.

**Mental health support** exists here but is thinner than at home, and worth locating before you need it rather than after.

## What actually works

Live somewhere that matches how you want to spend time. Learn the language. Join something with a schedule. Know your neighbours. Contribute to the community you moved into. Accept that people leave, and keep meeting new ones anyway.

## Common questions

### Is it easy to make friends in Bali?
It is easy to meet people and harder to build depth, because the foreign community turns over constantly. Communities organised around an activity last better than ones organised around nationality.

### Why does the banjar matter to a foreign resident?
It is the customary neighbourhood association with real authority over ceremonies, community obligations and local disputes, and it is the first forum when something goes wrong at your property.

### Should I learn Indonesian to live in Bali?
Yes. It is unusually learnable and a few months of effort changes how you are treated, what people tell you, and your position in every transaction you enter.

### What is the hardest part of living in Bali?
Transience. Long-term residents consistently name the constant departure of people they became close to as the most wearing part.

### Does community matter for property owners in Bali?
Directly. A villa whose owner is known locally gets cooperation on permits, early warning of problems and help resolving disputes. One whose owner nobody has met does not.
