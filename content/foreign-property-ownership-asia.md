Start from the rule rather than the exception. Across most of Southeast Asia, land is reserved for nationals. The differences are in what foreigners may hold instead.

## The comparison

| Country | Land | Building or unit | Term |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Indonesia | No | Hak Pakai, leasehold, HGB via company | 30 years, extendable |
| Thailand | No | Condominium freehold within a 49% building quota | Perpetual for the unit |
| Vietnam | No | Apartment ownership, quota limited | 50 years, renewal uncertain |
| Philippines | No | Up to 40% of a condominium building | Perpetual for the unit |
| Malaysia | **Yes**, with state approval and minimum values | Yes | Freehold or long leasehold |
| Japan | **Yes**, no nationality restriction | Yes | Freehold |
| Singapore | Restricted for landed; condominiums open | Yes | Freehold or 99-year |

Malaysia and Japan are the outliers. Everywhere else, a foreigner holds a unit, a lease or a right of use.

## What that means in practice

**Thailand** gives the cleanest ownership of a unit, inside a quota. You never own the land under the building.

**Vietnam** grants a defined term with renewal that is not guaranteed, which is a meaningful difference from Indonesia's statutory extension paths.

**The Philippines** works similarly to Thailand with a lower foreign quota.

**Japan** allows outright freehold with no restriction, which surprises people. The constraint there is not law, it is demographics and the fact that most buildings depreciate to near zero.

**Malaysia** permits freehold above state minimum values, and pairs it with a long-stay programme.

**Indonesia** is the most restrictive on paper and still attracts enormous foreign capital, which is the genuinely interesting question.

## So why does money keep choosing Bali?

Because ownership rights are not the only variable. Rental demand, cost of building, cost of living, climate, flight connections and the size of the resident foreign community all matter, and Bali scores well on every one of them.

Thailand offers better title and a more mature market. Japan offers real freehold and almost no rental upside outside the big cities. Malaysia offers ownership with far thinner tourism demand.

The trade people are actually making is weaker rights for stronger cash flow and a life they want.

## The comparison that matters

Not who lets you own, but what you are holding in year twenty-five and who can buy it from you. On that test, a Thai condominium and a Bali leasehold with a weak extension clause are very different assets, and the Bali one needs to earn considerably more to justify itself.

## Common questions

### Can foreigners own land in Asia?
Rarely. Malaysia and Japan are the main exceptions. Indonesia, Thailand, Vietnam and the Philippines all reserve land for nationals.

### Which Asian country is best for foreign property buyers?
For title security, Japan or Malaysia. For rental income and lifestyle, Bali or Thailand. The right answer depends on whether you want an asset or a business.

### Can foreigners own a condo in Thailand?
Yes, freehold, within a quota of 49 percent of a building's saleable area. The land beneath remains Thai-owned.

### How long can a foreigner hold property in Vietnam?
Typically 50 years for apartment ownership, with renewal that is not guaranteed in the way Indonesian extension paths are defined.

### Is Indonesia the most restrictive market in Asia?
On paper it is among the most restrictive for land. It also has some of the strongest tourism-driven rental demand, which is why capital keeps arriving despite the restriction.
