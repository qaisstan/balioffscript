Bali has an unusually large homeschooling and alternative education community, driven partly by cost and partly by the kind of family that moves here.

## Why people do it

**International school fees are the largest single expense** for most foreign families in Bali, frequently exceeding housing.

**The community exists.** Canggu, Ubud and Sanur all have established homeschool networks, co-ops, tutors and group activities, which removes the isolation that makes homeschooling hard elsewhere.

**Flexibility**, for families who travel or whose work moves.

**Alternative philosophies.** Bali attracts families interested in Montessori, Steiner, forest school and unschooling approaches, and there are established settings offering them.

## The legal position for foreign children

This is where people get confused, and the answer is simpler than expected.

**Your child's right to be in Indonesia runs through immigration, not education.** A foreign child needs a valid permit — typically a dependant permit attached to a parent's KITAS — regardless of whether they attend school, are homeschooled or do neither.

**Indonesian compulsory education law applies to Indonesian citizens.** A foreign child on a dependant permit is not within that framework in the same way, which is why homeschooling foreign children here is common and unremarkable.

**If one parent is Indonesian and the child holds Indonesian citizenship**, the position is different and worth taking advice on, because the child is within the Indonesian system.

## Accreditation, which is the real question

The legal position is straightforward. The practical one is not.

**What happens at fourteen, sixteen, eighteen?** A child needs recognised qualifications to enter university or employment, and "we homeschooled in Bali" is not a transcript.

The routes families use:

**An accredited distance programme** from the home country or an international provider, which issues recognised qualifications. This is the most common and most reliable answer, and it is what most families who homeschool seriously use.

**IGCSE and A-Level** or equivalent as external candidates, sat at an approved centre. Bali has options for this and it requires planning years ahead rather than arranging in the final year.

**Re-entering a formal school** for the final years, which many families do, and which is easier if the earlier years followed a recognisable curriculum.

**American high school diploma programmes** delivered online with accreditation.

The mistake is drifting through the early years without a plan and discovering at fifteen that there is no pathway to a qualification.

**Decide the endpoint first**, then work backwards. That single step separates the families for whom this works from the ones for whom it does not.

## The co-ops and hybrid options

Bali's middle ground is substantial and worth knowing about.

**Homeschool co-ops**, where groups of families share teaching, hire tutors and run group activities.

**Part-time enrolment** at some schools, where children attend for some subjects.

**Subject tutors**, which are affordable here in a way they are not at home — maths, science, languages — and which cover the areas parents cannot teach well.

**Activity-based groups**: surf, music, art, sport, Bahasa Indonesia.

For most families the workable version is not a parent teaching everything at a kitchen table. It is a distance curriculum for accreditation, tutors for the difficult subjects, and a co-op for social and group learning.

## The honest difficulties

**Social.** The Bali foreign community turns over constantly. Children make friends who leave, repeatedly, and that is harder on children than on adults.

**Parental time.** It is a substantial commitment, and it competes directly with running a business or a property here.

**Teenagers.** Homeschooling works better at primary age than through the teenage years, and many families who start intending to continue move to formal schooling around thirteen or fourteen.

**Gaps.** Without a structured curriculum, gaps accumulate quietly and surface at assessment.

## Cost

Substantially less than international school fees, which is the main driver.

A distance curriculum, a few tutors and co-op contributions is a fraction of a full international school place, and for families with more than one child the difference is very large.

## Common questions

### Is homeschooling legal in Bali for foreigners?
A foreign child's right to be in Indonesia runs through their immigration permit rather than education, and Indonesian compulsory education law applies to citizens, so homeschooling foreign children is common.

### What permit does a homeschooled child need in Indonesia?
The same as any foreign child — typically a dependant permit attached to a parent's KITAS — regardless of whether they attend school.

### How do homeschooled children get qualifications in Bali?
Through an accredited distance programme, sitting IGCSE or A-Levels as external candidates at an approved centre, an accredited online diploma, or re-entering formal school for the final years.

### Is there a homeschool community in Bali?
Yes, established networks and co-ops in Canggu, Ubud and Sanur, with shared teaching, tutors and group activities.

### When does homeschooling get harder in Bali?
Through the teenage years. Many families who start at primary age move to formal schooling around thirteen or fourteen, and planning the qualification route early is what makes that transition work.
