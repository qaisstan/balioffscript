The single most common finding in Bali land due diligence is that the parcel is not the size everyone thought it was. A survey costs a few hundred dollars and it is skipped constantly.

## Why the numbers differ

**Old measurement.** Many Bali parcels were measured decades ago with methods that do not match modern survey accuracy.

**Informal family division.** Land passed through generations and divided by agreement, with fences and walls placed where they made sense rather than where a plan said.

**Encroachment.** A neighbour's wall, building or planting extending over the line, sometimes for years, sometimes deliberately.

**Erosion**, on coastal and riverside parcels, where the boundary is defined by a physical feature that has moved.

**Setbacks and access strips** that exist on the ground but were never reflected in the record.

**Simple error**, in a system with a great deal of manual record keeping.

The result is that the advertised area, the certificate area and the fenced area routinely differ, and the difference can be material.

## What a survey establishes

**The actual measured area** of the parcel.

**Where the boundaries physically are**, marked on the ground.

**Whether the certificate plan matches reality**, and where it does not.

**Encroachments**, in either direction.

**The relationship to setbacks** — road, boundary, river, coastal — and therefore what is genuinely buildable.

**Levels and slope**, which matter enormously for build cost on anything other than flat land.

## Why it matters commercially, not just legally

**You are paying per are.** If the parcel is 4.2 are rather than the 5 are advertised, you are overpaying by sixteen percent. That is a legitimate and entirely factual basis for renegotiating.

**Buildable area is what you are actually buying.** Subtract setbacks and any access strip, then apply KDB, and the number that remains determines what you can build. A large parcel with a river setback running through it may support less than a smaller clean one.

**A boundary dispute is expensive and slow.** Indonesian litigation is lengthy and enforcement is uncertain, so the encroachment you discover now and resolve at the negotiating table is vastly cheaper than the one you discover after you own it.

**Resale.** A buyer's surveyor will find what yours would have, and you will be the one discounting.

## Getting one done

Use a **licensed surveyor**, engaged by you, not by the seller or introduced by the agent.

Have them work from the **certificate and the cadastral plan** obtained from the land office, not from a copy the seller provides.

Ask for **physical boundary markers** placed, and photograph them.

Ask for **levels** if the site is not flat, because retaining and foundation costs on a slope are a large part of a build budget and cannot be estimated from a photograph.

Have the surveyor's report **in writing**, with the measured area stated.

## Have the neighbours present

This is the Bali-specific part and it matters.

Boundary determination here works far better with the adjoining owners present and agreeing. A boundary marked with the neighbour standing there nodding is a boundary that does not become a dispute in three years.

A boundary marked from a plan, with nobody consulted, is an invitation.

Your notary or surveyor can arrange this, and the **banjar** will help. Take the time.

## What to do with the result

**If the area is smaller than advertised**, renegotiate on the per-are price. This is factual, documented and hard to argue with.

**If there is an encroachment**, resolve it before completing — either the seller deals with it or the price reflects it.

**If the certificate does not match the ground**, understand why before proceeding. Sometimes it is a correctable record error, sometimes it indicates a division nobody documented, which is a different and larger problem.

**If setbacks remove more than expected**, recalculate your build before deciding whether the parcel still works.

## The cost comparison

A survey on an ordinary parcel costs a few hundred dollars.

The last three boundary problems I am aware of cost their owners, respectively: a renegotiated price they never got, a wall rebuilt after a dispute, and a build redesigned after a setback was discovered post-purchase.

It is the cheapest line in due diligence and the one most frequently cut.

## Common questions

### Do I need a survey to buy land in Bali?
Yes. The advertised area, the certificate area and what is physically fenced routinely differ, and the difference is a legitimate basis for renegotiating the price.

### Why is Bali land often a different size than advertised?
Old measurement methods, informal family division over generations, encroachment, erosion on coastal parcels, and manual record keeping all contribute.

### What should a Bali land survey include?
Measured area, physical boundary markers, comparison against the certificate and cadastral plan, encroachments, setback impact on buildable area, and levels on sloped sites.

### Should neighbours be present at a boundary survey?
Yes. A boundary agreed with adjoining owners present is one that does not become a dispute later, and the banjar can help arrange it.

### How much does a land survey cost in Bali?
A few hundred dollars on an ordinary parcel, which is the cheapest item in due diligence and the one most often skipped.
