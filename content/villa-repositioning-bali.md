A villa running at fifty-five percent occupancy in a street where comparable properties run at seventy-five is not a bad villa. It is a villa with three or four fixable problems, and they are almost always the same ones.

## First, find out if there is actually a problem

**Compare properly.** Watch four or five genuinely comparable villas — same street, same bedroom count, same price band — and track their availability calendars over six to eight weeks. Record which dates fill and at what price.

That tells you what a property like yours actually achieves. Your monthly statement tells you what happened; the comparison tells you what should have.

If you are within a few points of comparable properties, the property is fine and the market is what it is. If you are twenty points below, keep reading.

## The order to fix things

This is deliberate. Doing it in a different order wastes money.

**One: photography.** The cheapest and highest-return fix available, and the one most owners have never redone. A guest decides in about a second on one image against fifty alternatives. Professional photography, shot in early morning or the hour before sunset, with the pool leading and an evening shot included, routinely moves bookings twenty to thirty percent on the same property.

**Two: the listing order.** Free. Most listings bury the best image at position nine. The first five carry almost all the weight: pool with villa, main living space, best bedroom, evening or drone shot, bathroom. Reorder it this afternoon.

**Three: pricing.** One annual rate is the most common error. Bali has four genuine seasons, and the annual number is decided in February, March and November, not August. Set seasonal bands, check your floor — the rate below which a booking costs you money — and use monthly rates through the trough.

**Four: response time and reviews.** Platform ranking rewards both. A manager responding in hours rather than minutes is costing you visibility.

**Five: the garden.** Cheaper than interior work and it appears in every photograph. Clean up the planting, add structure and a few mature specimens, install lighting — then rephotograph.

**Six: the obvious defects.** Tired pool surface, failing aircon, stained grout, dead plants. These generate the reviews that suppress everything else.

**Seven: interior refresh.** Paint, soft furnishings, styling. Worth doing, and after the six cheaper things above.

Notice that the first four cost almost nothing.

## The manager question

The most common cause of a twenty-point occupancy gap is a manager who performed adequately at first and then quietly drifted.

Nobody tells you, because their incentive is a quiet life rather than the marginal booking. Owners who read monthly statements never notice; owners who compare against the market catch it in one season.

**Before replacing them**, put the comparison in front of them and ask what they intend to do about it. A good manager responds with a plan. A drifting one responds with reasons the market is difficult.

**If you replace them**, read the agreement first — notice period, termination cost, and crucially **who controls forward bookings on exit**. Some agreements are drafted so a departing manager keeps or cancels them, which is leverage you do not want them to have.

## Repositioning the product

Sometimes the property is genuinely aimed at the wrong guest.

**A five-bedroom in a nomad area** is competing for a guest who is not booking. Consider long-term rental instead, which in residential areas frequently nets similarly with a fraction of the workload.

**A small unit with no view in an emerging corridor** competes on price alone against a much larger market. Adding a view, a better pool relationship or genuine outdoor living changes what it is.

**A villa with no workspace in Canggu or Pererenan** is missing the segment that fills February and March. A proper desk and chair, stated internet speed and a monthly rate is a small change with a disproportionate effect.

## The one that stops everything

**Licensing.** Before increasing marketing activity, confirm the zoning permits commercial accommodation, the building has PBG and SLF, and the operating position is in order — noting Bali closed new foreign-owned villa and homestay registration to PMA companies on 22 July 2026.

Repositioning a property into greater visibility while its licensing position is unresolved is making a quiet problem loud.

## What to expect

Photography, listing order, seasonal pricing and response time, done together, commonly move a property from fifty-five percent to sixty-five or seventy within two seasons.

That is a thirty percent revenue increase for a few thousand dollars and some attention, which is a better return than anything else available to an existing owner.

## Common questions

### Why is my Bali villa not getting bookings?
Most commonly photography, a single annual rate, slow response times and a manager who has quietly drifted — in that order, and all cheap to fix.

### What is the highest-return fix for an underperforming villa?
Professional photography, which routinely moves bookings twenty to thirty percent on the same property and is the thing most owners have never redone.

### How do I know if my villa is underperforming?
Track four or five comparable villas' availability calendars over six to eight weeks. If you are twenty points below them on occupancy, there is a fixable problem.

### Should I replace an underperforming villa manager?
Put the comparison to them first and see whether they respond with a plan. Before replacing, check the agreement's notice, termination cost and who controls forward bookings on exit.

### Can an underperforming villa be repositioned for a different guest?
Yes. A property aimed at the wrong market — a large villa in a nomad area, or one with no workspace in Canggu — can be redirected to long-term rental or to the segment that actually books there.
