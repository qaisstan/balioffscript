Bali is a relatively safe place and villa break-ins do happen. Almost all of them are opportunistic rather than planned, which means the response is prevention rather than defence.

## What actually gets taken

Phones, laptops, cameras, cash and passports left visible. Bags near an open door. Scooters. Items from an unsecured outdoor area.

The pattern is someone walking past an open gate or an unlocked sliding door and taking what is within reach. Forced entry is far less common than opportunity.

That tells you where to spend.

## The layers that matter

**A perimeter.** A wall or fence with a gate that closes and locks. This does more than anything else, because it removes the opportunity before it reaches the building.

**Lighting.** Motion-activated lights around the perimeter and entrances. Cheap, effective, and it also improves the property at night for guests.

**Locks that work.** Sliding doors in Bali villas are frequently the weak point — many can be lifted out of the track or opened with minimal effort. Proper locks and anti-lift blocks cost very little.

**A safe.** Bolted, in a wardrobe, large enough for a laptop. Guests use them if they exist and leave valuables on the bed if they do not. This is the single highest-return item for guest-facing properties.

**Cameras** at the entrance and perimeter, not inside. Be explicit in the listing about where cameras are — undisclosed interior cameras are a serious breach of guest trust and of platform rules, and they end badly.

**Someone present.** A night security presence, or a caretaker living on site, which is common in Bali and which deters almost everything.

## Staff versus service

**Employing security directly** brings the full Indonesian employment framework — BPJS, THR, overtime, leave, and the termination provisions foreign employers consistently underestimate. Manageable, and a permanent commitment.

**A security service** costs more per hour and the employment obligations sit with them.

**A caretaker or manager living on site** is the arrangement most Bali villas use and it works well, covering security, maintenance and guest response in one relationship.

For a single villa, the on-site caretaker arrangement is usually the right answer.

## The empty period problem

A villa between guests, or through a quiet February, is when most losses happen.

**Never leave it genuinely empty.** Someone should be attending daily — the pool and garden service does this naturally, which is one more argument for keeping it running through quiet periods.

**Timer lighting**, so the property does not read as vacant at night.

**Check your insurance unoccupancy clause.** Many policies restrict cover when a property is empty beyond a stated period, which means an empty villa can fall outside cover exactly when it is most exposed. Read the clause rather than assuming.

## The guest side

**Tell guests what to do.** Where the safe is, that the gate should be closed, that valuables should not be left visible. A short note in the welcome pack costs nothing.

**Do not hold passports.** Some operators do and it creates problems. Guests should hold their own documents in the safe.

**Have a reachable contact** at any hour. A guest with a problem and nobody to call writes a review about it.

**Key handling.** Know who holds keys — manager, staff, previous guests. Rekey when staff change, which owners almost never do.

## What not to bother with

**Heavy fortification.** Bars on every window make a villa feel like a prison and it shows in photographs, which costs you bookings. The deterrent value beyond a decent perimeter and good locks is marginal.

**Interior cameras.** A serious guest-trust problem, a platform policy breach, and legally fraught. Do not.

**Complex alarm systems** that nobody monitors. An alarm sounding in an empty street achieves nothing. Presence achieves everything.

## The community layer

This is the Bali-specific point and it is underrated.

A villa in a street where the neighbours know who you are, where you have introduced yourself to the **banjar**, and where staff are part of the local community, is a property that people look out for.

A villa owned by an absent foreigner nobody has met, staffed by people brought in from elsewhere, is not.

That relationship is the cheapest security available and it is built before anything happens, not after.

## Common questions

### Is Bali safe for villa owners?
Relatively safe, with most incidents opportunistic rather than planned — someone taking what is visible through an open gate or unlocked door rather than forcing entry.

### What is the best security for a Bali villa?
A locking perimeter gate, motion lighting, proper locks on sliding doors, a bolted safe, and someone present on site. Presence deters more than hardware.

### Should I put cameras in my villa?
At the entrance and perimeter, disclosed in the listing. Never inside — undisclosed interior cameras breach guest trust and platform rules and end badly.

### Does insurance cover theft from an empty villa?
Many policies restrict cover when a property is unoccupied beyond a stated period. Read the unoccupancy clause rather than assuming you are covered.

### Should I employ security staff directly?
Direct employment brings the full Indonesian labour framework including severance obligations. For a single villa, an on-site caretaker or manager covering security, maintenance and guest response is usually the better arrangement.
