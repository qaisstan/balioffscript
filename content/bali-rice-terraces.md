Rice terraces are the image of Bali, and the two most famous sites offer completely different versions of it.

## Tegalalang

Twenty minutes north of Ubud, a steep narrow valley of terraces with cafes on the rim, swings out over the drop, nests, hearts and every other photo installation.

**What it is good for:** convenience, a dramatic photograph, an easy morning from Ubud.

**What it is not:** peaceful, authentic or large. It is a small valley with a lot of infrastructure built for photographs.

**How to do it well:** arrive between 6.30 and 8am. The light is better, the buses have not come, the swings are empty and the entry is a fraction of the experience it becomes by ten.

**Costs:** an entry fee, parking, and then small payments to individual landowners as you walk down through the terraces, which is normal and locally agreed rather than a scam. The swings are separately priced and vary widely.

## Jatiluwih

An hour and a half from Ubud, on the slopes below Batukaru, a UNESCO World Heritage site covering hundreds of hectares of terraces.

**What it is good for:** scale, walking, quiet, and the actual agricultural landscape rather than a photo stop.

**What it is:** vast, gently sloping, with several marked walking routes from 30 minutes to three hours, a handful of simple cafes, and a fraction of Tegalalang's crowds.

**The subak system** that manages the water here is the reason for the UNESCO listing: a cooperative irrigation network run through water temples for a thousand years, in which each farmer's allocation is decided collectively. It is one of the more remarkable pieces of social engineering in Asia and it is still in daily use.

**Costs:** an entry fee per person plus parking, collected at a gate.

## The others

**Pupuan and Belimbing**, in Tabanan, on the road to the north coast. Terraces at scale, no tourism at all, and a spectacular drive.

**Sidemen**, in the east, where the terraces climb toward Agung and you can walk between them freely with no gate and no fee.

**Tirta Gangga surrounds**, where the water palace sits among working fields.

**Munduk**, where the terraces are interspersed with clove and coffee.

**Antosari and the west**, empty and unvisited.

## The seasonal question nobody asks

Rice terraces look completely different depending on the cycle, and each field is on its own schedule.

- **Just planted:** flooded, mirror-like, reflecting sky. The best photographs.
- **Growing:** the bright green of the postcards.
- **Ready to harvest:** gold, heavier, beautiful in late light.
- **Just harvested:** stubble and mud, which is what half the visitors who complain about rice terraces actually saw.

Bali grows two to three crops a year and different valleys are at different stages at any time, which means there is always somewhere green. Ask a driver where the fields are good this week; they will know.

## Etiquette

You are walking on somebody's working farm. Stay on the paths between plots, do not walk on the dividing bunds where rice is planted, do not fly drones low over people working, and pay the small collections asked for along the way. If someone is working, ask before photographing them.

## How to build a day around them

**From Ubud, half a day:** Tegalalang at 6.30am, then Tirta Empul at opening, back for a late breakfast. Both are within 30 minutes of the town.

**From Ubud, full day:** north through Payangan to Jatiluwih, two hours walking in the terraces, lunch at one of the simple cafes on the rim, then Ulun Danu Beratan or the twin lakes on the way home.

**From the south:** treat Jatiluwih as a full day with a driver and combine it with Tanah Lot at sunset on the way back down the west coast. Tegalalang from Canggu is three hours of driving for a 40-minute stop, which is not a good trade.

**From Sidemen:** walk out of your hotel. The terraces there are working farmland with no gate, no fee and nobody else in them, which for many people is the version they wanted all along.

## What to bring

Shoes with grip, because the bunds and paths are narrow and slippery when wet. Water, because there is little shade in the middle of a terrace. Small notes for the collections along the paths. And a hat: this is open farmland eight degrees from the equator and the sun is direct by nine in the morning.

## Common questions

### Is Tegalalang worth visiting?
Early in the morning, yes. By mid-morning it is a queue for a swing. Jatiluwih is the better landscape.

### How much does Jatiluwih cost?
An entry fee per person plus parking, paid at the gate in cash. It includes access to the walking trails.

### Which rice terrace is UNESCO listed?
Jatiluwih, as part of the subak cultural landscape listing, along with several other subak sites.

### When is the best time of day to see rice terraces?
Early morning for light, temperature and quiet. Late afternoon works for photographs but the light goes fast this close to the equator.

### Can you walk in Jatiluwih rice terraces?
Yes, on marked trails from 30 minutes to around three hours, which is the main reason to choose it over Tegalalang.
