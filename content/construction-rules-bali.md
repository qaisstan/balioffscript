Bali constrains building more than most of Southeast Asia, and the constraints come from three separate places: national and provincial regulation, the regency spatial plan, and the customary rules of the village you are building in. All three bind.

## The height limit

Bali applies a building height limit that is famously tied to the height of a coconut palm and is generally applied at fifteen metres. In practice this means most buildings are limited to roughly three storeys.

It is the rule most responsible for Bali looking the way it does, and it is enforced. Exceptions exist for specific structures and specific zones, but they are exceptions, and a design that assumes one will be granted is a design at risk.

The practical effect on a villa project is that you build outward, not upward. Land area, not floor count, determines what you can deliver. That is why plot size matters so much more here than in markets where you can add a storey.

## Coverage: KDB and KLB

**KDB**, the building coverage ratio, caps the proportion of your plot that the building footprint may occupy. Depending on the zone this commonly sits in the range of forty to sixty percent, and lower in green and agricultural zones.

**KLB**, the floor area ratio, caps total floor area across all storeys relative to plot size.

These two decide the real capacity of your land. A five are plot in a zone with a fifty percent KDB gives you 250 square metres of footprint, and the rest must stay open. That open area is your pool, garden and parking — which is fine, because those are what a Bali villa sells on.

Buyers routinely calculate a build from plot size alone and are then surprised by the KDB. Do it the other way: check the zone's KDB and KLB first, then design.

## Setbacks

Several apply at once.

**Road setback** — distance from the road boundary, varying with the road's classification.

**Boundary setbacks** — distance from neighbouring parcels, particularly where there are openings.

**Sempadan pantai** — the coastal setback from the high water line, applying to any coastal parcel.

**Sempadan sungai** — the river setback, which catches more Canggu and Pererenan parcels than people expect, and is strictly applied.

Together these can remove a surprising amount of a plot from use, especially on narrow or irregularly shaped land. Have them drawn on a site plan before you buy, not after.

## The customary layer

This is the part that does not appear in any spatial plan and it is real.

Every location in Bali sits within a *desa adat*, a customary village, with its own rules. These can govern building close to a temple, building heights and orientation near sacred sites, the timing of works around ceremonies, and obligations to the community.

There is also *tri mandala*, the spatial ordering principle that positions the sacred, the human and the service areas of a site relative to the mountain and the sea. Good architects working in Bali design with it. Ignoring it produces buildings that sit badly and, more practically, can generate objections.

Practically: establish a relationship with the *banjar*, the local community organisation, before you start. Contribute to community obligations. Ask what the local rules are rather than assuming there are none. A project with community support proceeds. One without it encounters an endless supply of difficulty, none of which is written down anywhere you can appeal to.

## Working hours and ceremonies

Construction pauses around major ceremonies, and on **Nyepi**, the day of silence, everything stops island-wide. Local ceremonies at the village level also halt work, sometimes for several days at a time.

Build this into the schedule. A programme that assumes continuous work will overrun, and blaming the contractor for a ceremony is not a productive conversation.

## What to establish before you buy

Zone and permitted use for the exact parcel. KDB and KLB for that zone. All applicable setbacks drawn on a site plan. River and coastal proximity. Distance to the nearest temple and any resulting constraint. Which desa adat the land falls under, and whether anyone has spoken to them.

Every one of these is cheap to check before purchase and expensive to discover afterwards.

## Common questions

### What is the building height limit in Bali?
Generally fifteen metres, tied by custom to the height of a coconut palm, which limits most buildings to around three storeys. It is enforced.

### What is KDB in Bali?
The building coverage ratio, capping the proportion of a plot the building footprint may occupy. It varies by zone, commonly forty to sixty percent, and is lower in green and agricultural zones.

### What is the river setback in Bali?
The sempadan sungai, a mandatory distance from a river boundary. It catches more Canggu and Pererenan parcels than buyers expect and is strictly applied.

### Do I need permission from the local village to build in Bali?
There is a customary layer, the desa adat, with its own rules on building near temples, orientation, ceremony timing and community obligations. It is not in the spatial plan and it is real.

### Does construction stop for ceremonies in Bali?
Yes. Work pauses island-wide for Nyepi and locally for village ceremonies, sometimes for several days. Build it into the programme rather than treating it as delay.
