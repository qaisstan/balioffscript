Can a Bali lease run 80 years?

Indonesian practice allows leasehold arrangements reaching about 80 years, but almost no notary will write it as one term. Here is how it is actually built.

By Kai, Bali property adviser Updated 2026-09-16 2 min read

On this page

  1. How the 80 years is assembled
  2. Why that distinction is the whole thing
  3. What to check on any long-lease offer
  4. The prepaid question
  5. Common questions

Listings advertise "80 year leasehold" constantly. The number is achievable and the way it is reached matters more than the headline.

How the 80 years is assembled

Not as a single term. The common structure is:

StageTypical
Initial term30 years
First extension25 years
Second extension25 years
Totalaround 80 years

Most Bali notaries decline to draft a single term beyond 30 years, following the general direction of Indonesian land law on foreign control of land. So the long horizon is built from an initial term plus contractual extensions.

Why that distinction is the whole thing

An 80-year lease sold as one block implies 80 years of certainty. What you actually hold is 30 years of certainty plus two extension rights, and those rights are only worth what the clause makes them worth.

If the extensions say prioritas perpanjangan, priority rather than guarantee, you do not have 80 years. You have 30 years and two conversations.

If they say jaminan perpanjangan with a stated term and a stated price or formula, the 80 is real.

What to check on any long-lease offer

  • Is it drafted as 30 plus extensions, or as a single term?
  • Does each extension say jaminan or prioritas?
  • Is the extension price a figure, a formula, or left open?
  • Is there a defined notice window for each extension?
  • Does it bind heirs and successors, for all three stages?
  • Who pays the taxes and fees on each extension?

That last one is quietly expensive. An extension can attract its own costs, and a clause silent on who bears them creates an argument at renewal.

The prepaid question

Some long leases are paid entirely up front for the full horizon. Others pay the initial term and price the extensions later.

Paying up front removes the price risk and increases what you lose if the underlying title turns out to be defective. Paying in stages keeps money back and leaves you exposed to a repriced extension.

Neither is automatically right. What matters is that you know which one you signed.
