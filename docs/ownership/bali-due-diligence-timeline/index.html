How long does due diligence take in Bali?

A clean title takes one to three weeks to verify properly. Anyone pressuring you inside that window is telling you something about the deal.

By Kai, Strategic Investment Adviser Updated 2026-09-21 5 min read

Due diligence in Bali is not a formality and it is not fast. It is the part of the transaction that decides whether you own something, and the pressure to skip it is constant.

Why it takes as long as it does

Nothing here is instant because nothing here is digitised end to end. Verification means a person physically attending the land office, requesting records, comparing them to the certificate in front of them and confirming the parcel on the ground matches the parcel on paper.

Each of those steps has a queue. None of them can be accelerated by paying more, and anyone who says otherwise is selling you a document rather than a check.

The realistic schedule

Week one: title and identity.

The notary requests the land office record for the certificate and compares it line by line with the original document the seller has produced. Name, parcel number, area, boundaries, registered encumbrances. They confirm the person selling is the person on the certificate, or holds a valid, current power of attorney from them.

They also check whether the seller is married, because spousal consent is required to dispose of jointly held property and its absence voids the transfer.

This week alone kills more deals than every other stage combined, usually because the area on the ground does not match the certificate or the seller is not the registered owner.

Week one to two: planning and permits.

Zoning confirmed against the regional spatial plan for the specific parcel, not the area. KDB and KLB for the zone. Applicable setbacks — road, boundary, river, coast — drawn against the parcel boundary.

For a property with a building: PBG and SLF located and checked against what physically stands there. A building larger than its approval, or a building with no approval at all, is a liability you would be buying.

Week two: encumbrances and third parties.

Existing mortgages, caveats or registered rights. Any current lease, tenancy or management agreement, all of which bind you. Access rights over land you do not own, and whether they exist in a document or only in practice. Unpaid PBB, which transfers with the land. Outstanding contractor claims.

Week two to three: physical and local.

A site visit with the notary or surveyor, comparing the parcel boundary to what is fenced and built. Enquiry with the banjar about any customary constraint, community obligation or known dispute. This is the step most often skipped by foreigners and it surfaces things no document search will.

Then the deed.

Only once all of that is clean does money move against a signed deed.

What extends it

Land held under an inheritance not yet formally divided. A certificate held by a bank against a loan. A seller abroad, which means a power of attorney has to be executed and legalised. Parcels that have been split or merged and whose records lag. Anything in an area with known boundary disputes. Any of these can add two to six weeks, and none of them are unusual.

What should never compress it

"Another buyer is ready to pay today." The oldest pressure in this market, and it is almost always untrue. If it is true, let them.

"The notary already checked it." Which notary? Engaged by whom? A check run by the seller's notary is not a check run for you.

"We can do the deed now and sort the permit after." You will own the problem and the leverage will be gone.

A deposit "to take it off the market." If you must, make it small, hold it with your own notary rather than the agent or seller, and make it refundable in writing against defined conditions. A deposit paid to an agent's personal account is gone.

The written output

At the end you should hold a written due diligence report from a notary you engaged, covering title verification at the land office, seller identity and authority, zoning and permitted use, permits, encumbrances, access, tax status and any customary constraint.

If nobody produces a document, no due diligence happened. A verbal reassurance that "it is all fine" is not a deliverable and is not something you can rely on later.

What it costs

Proportionate to the purchase and small against it. On a USD 200,000 property, budget roughly USD 1,500 to USD 3,000. On a USD 500,000 property, USD 5,000 to USD 8,000 for a thorough job including survey and full contract review.

Every buyer who has lost money in this market saved that amount first.

Common questions

How long does due diligence take in Bali?

One to three weeks for a clean title, and four to eight weeks where there is an undivided inheritance, an encumbrance, a seller abroad or a records discrepancy.

Can I use the seller's notary in Bali?

You can, and you should not. The seller's notary is the seller's adviser. Engaging your own is the only independent check in the entire transaction.

What does due diligence cost in Bali?

Roughly USD 1,500 to USD 3,000 on a USD 200,000 property, and USD 5,000 to USD 8,000 on a USD 500,000 property including survey and full contract review.

What is the most common problem due diligence finds?

The area on the ground not matching the certificate, or the person selling not being the registered owner and not holding a valid power of attorney from them.

Should I pay a deposit before due diligence is complete?

Only if it is small, held by your own notary rather than the agent or seller, and refundable in writing against defined conditions.
