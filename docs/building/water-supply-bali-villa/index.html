Where does a Bali villa get its water?

Piped supply does not reach everywhere, wells need permits, and on the Bukit water is a genuine operating cost rather than a detail.

By Kai, Bali property adviser Updated 2026-09-21 4 min read

Water is the constraint that decides whether a Bali property works, and it is checked less often than almost anything else on a due diligence list.

The three sources

PDAM, the municipal supply. Piped, metered, and cheap where it reaches. Coverage is good in Denpasar, Sanur, Seminyak, Kuta and the developed parts of Badung, and patchy to absent elsewhere. Pressure and continuity vary, and in peak season some areas see reduced supply.

A bore or well. Groundwater, pumped. Common across Bali and the default in areas PDAM does not reach. Extraction is regulated and requires a permit — this is not optional and it is enforced with increasing attention, because groundwater depletion is a genuine issue on the island.

Trucked water. Delivered by tanker into a storage tank. The fallback where neither of the above works, and the standard arrangement across much of the Bukit.

Most properties end up on a combination, with storage tanks buffering whichever source is primary.

The Bukit problem

The Bukit peninsula is limestone. Groundwater sits deep, boreholes are expensive and not always successful, and PDAM coverage is inconsistent.

A large number of Bukit villas run on trucked water, and that is a real recurring operating cost rather than a detail. It is also a genuine operational risk: in peak season, when every villa on the peninsula wants water at once and every pool needs topping up, delivery schedules slip.

A five-bedroom villa with a pool, running at capacity in August, consumes a lot of water. A villa that runs dry has guests who leave reviews about it.

Before buying anything on the Bukit, establish in writing: where the water comes from, what it costs annually, and what happens in August. Vague answers here are the single most useful warning sign in that area.

What to check anywhere

The actual source, confirmed rather than described. See the meter, the bore or the tank.

The permit, if it is a bore. Groundwater extraction without one is a compliance problem attaching to the property.

Storage capacity. How many days of supply does the tank hold at full occupancy? Two days is thin, a week is comfortable.

Pressure at the highest point of the building, tested. Ground floor pressure tells you nothing about the upstairs shower.

Annual cost, itemised, from actual invoices rather than an estimate.

Peak season behaviour, from the previous owner or manager, specifically.

Water quality, and what treatment is installed. Bore water in Bali frequently needs filtration, and in some coastal areas salinity is an issue.

What it costs

PDAM where available is inexpensive and the cheapest option by a wide margin.

A bore is a capital cost — drilling, pump, permit — then running cost is electricity.

Trucked water is the most expensive option on an ongoing basis, and the cost scales directly with occupancy. A busy villa costs more to water than an empty one, which is worth building into your operating model rather than treating as a fixed cost.

Building on unserviced land

If you are buying land to build, water availability determines whether the project is viable at all, and it should be established before you commit rather than during design.

Confirm whether PDAM can connect and what it costs. If not, confirm whether a bore is likely to succeed at that location and whether a permit is obtainable. Neighbours' experience is the best available evidence.

Design in adequate storage. Tank capacity is cheap at the build stage and expensive to retrofit.

The wider point

Bali's water situation is under genuine pressure from development, tourism and agriculture, and this is not a static picture. Groundwater extraction is more regulated than it was, and that direction is unlikely to reverse.

A property whose water supply depends on an unpermitted bore is carrying a risk that grows rather than shrinks.

Common questions

Where does a Bali villa get its water?

From PDAM municipal supply where it reaches, from a permitted bore or well, or from trucked deliveries into a storage tank. Many properties use a combination.

Do I need a permit for a well in Bali?

Yes. Groundwater extraction is regulated and requires a permit, and enforcement has increased as groundwater depletion has become a recognised issue.

Why is water a problem on the Bukit?

The peninsula is limestone, so groundwater is deep and boreholes are expensive and uncertain, while PDAM coverage is inconsistent. Many properties run on trucked water.

How much does trucked water cost in Bali?

It is the most expensive option and the cost scales with occupancy, so a busy villa costs meaningfully more to supply than an empty one.

What should I check about water before buying?

The actual source and its permit, storage capacity in days at full occupancy, pressure at the highest point of the building, annual cost from real invoices, and peak season behaviour.
