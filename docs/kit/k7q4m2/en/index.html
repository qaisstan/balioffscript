How to use this kit

Most Bali deals do not go wrong on price. They go wrong because one link in a chain does not match the others, and nobody checked the chain in order.

This kit is the order. Work through it with the actual property in front of you: the certificate, the zoning, the lease draft, the rental figures you were shown. Wherever you cannot tick a line, you have found the question to ask next.

Three rules sit above everything in here:

  • Your own notary. Not the seller's, not the agent's, not the developer's. A notary (PPAT) the other side brought is working for the other side.
  • Nothing verbal exists. If a promise is not in the deed or the lease, it was never made.
  • Speed is a tactic. Nothing legitimate in an Indonesian land transfer requires you to skip a step this week.

1. The chain: nine links that all have to hold

A property in Bali is only as good as the weakest of these. A break anywhere invalidates everything after it.

LinkWhat you are checkingWhere it usually breaks
1. LandThe certificate exists, is genuine and matches the plot you walkedPhotocopies, double certificates, a plot smaller than advertised
2. OwnerThe person signing has the authority to signFamily land with heirs who never agreed
3. AgreementThe lease or deed gives you what you think it gives youExtension "promised" but not priced or binding
4. ZoningThe spatial plan allows what you want to do thereGreen or yellow zone sold as a rental investment
5. Permitted useBuilding and operating are both allowed at that densityKDB caps that make the plan impossible
6. Business classificationThe KBLI code for your rental model is open to youClassifications closed to new foreign companies
7. PBGEvery structure on the plot has a building approvalExtensions and pool houses built without one
8. SLFThe finished building is certified fit for use"Being processed" for years
9. Operating licenceThe villa may legally take paying guestsA legal building with an illegal rental

A beautiful villa with a clean title is still a bad investment if link 4, 6 or 9 is broken. Those three are the ones nobody photographs.

3. Twenty questions to ask before you pay anything

Ask them in writing. Keep the answers. Vague answers are answers too.

About the land and the owner

1. Can my notary see the original certificate this week, and check it at BPN before I pay a deposit?

2. Who exactly owns the land, and is anyone else entitled to it, a spouse, children, siblings, a family temple?

3. Has the land ever been inherited, split or merged? Can I see the old certificates?

4. Is the access road a registered right of way, and how wide is it on paper?

5. Is there any mortgage, lien or dispute registered against this land?

About the zoning and the permits

6. What is the RDTR zoning for this exact parcel, and can I see it on the official map?

7. What KDB and KLB apply, and does the current building comply?

8. Does every structure have a PBG? Can I see each one?

9. Is the SLF issued, not applied for?

10. Are there beach, river, cliff or temple setbacks on this plot?

About the lease or the deal

11. How many years are left on the lease from the date I sign, not from the date it started?

12. Is the extension priced or formula-based, and is it binding on the owner's heirs?

13. Can I sell, transfer or sublease my remaining years without the owner's approval?

14. What happens to the buildings when the lease ends?

15. Who pays which taxes on this transaction? Can that go in writing?

About the money

16. What is the rental model, nightly, monthly or long-term, and is it permitted on this plot?

17. Which business classification will the rental run under, and is it open today?

18. Can I see real booking data or bank statements, not a projection?

19. What are the management fee, the staffing cost and the platform commission?

20. Off-plan: what happens to my money if the project stops, and where is it held until each stage is built?

4. Twelve lease clauses to insist on

A lease is the most common way foreigners hold property in Bali. It is also where most of the value is won or lost.

1. Term start date. The lease runs from the date you sign, not from an earlier date in someone else's agreement.

2. Priced extension. A fixed price or a written formula for the extension, not "to be negotiated".

3. Binding successors. The extension and every other right bind the owner's heirs, successors and anyone they sell to.

4. Right to transfer. You may sell or assign your remaining years without needing the owner's consent.

5. Right to sublease. You may rent the property to others, for the rental model you intend.

6. Right to build and renovate. Within the zoning, with the owner's cooperation on permits written in.

7. Permit cooperation. The owner must sign what the permit offices require, within a set time.

8. End-of-term buildings. What happens to the structures when the lease ends, stated plainly.

9. Taxes. Who pays the land tax and any transaction taxes, for the whole term.

10. Registration. The lease is made before a notary and registered, not a private agreement.

11. Default and remedies. What happens if the owner breaks the agreement, and what you can recover.

12. Disputes. Which law, which court or arbitration, and in which language the binding version is written.

5. Fifteen red flags

Any one of these is a reason to slow down. Two or more is usually a reason to walk away.

  • "Freehold in your name." Foreigners cannot hold Hak Milik under any structure.
  • A nominee: an Indonesian holding the land "for you" with a private side agreement.
  • Pressure to pay a deposit before your notary has seen the original certificate.
  • The seller's notary offered as "the notary".
  • A price far below the area, explained by a story rather than a document.
  • Yields quoted gross and without a breakdown of costs.
  • Rental income "guaranteed" by the developer, with no security behind the guarantee.
  • Off-plan payments that go to the developer with no link to construction stages.
  • A developer that leases the land itself on a short lease.
  • Zoning confirmed verbally or with a screenshot nobody can trace.
  • Structures with no PBG, "which everyone has here".
  • An extension clause that says "may be extended by agreement".
  • Cash payments, or money routed through personal accounts.
  • A company for sale with no LKPM reports filed.
  • An agent who gets defensive when you ask for any of the documents above.

The agency brochure for this villa would say 14.2% (gross income divided by price). Before tax and before the lease expiring, it is 5.5%. Counting the lease you are using up, it is 1.5%.

That does not make it a bad deal. It makes it a different deal from the one in the brochure. A villa can still make sense for the life it gives you, for capital growth on a long lease, or for the rental model that suits it. Just make the decision on the real number.

Income tax depends on how you hold the property and on your tax residence. Get that line from your own tax consultant, in Indonesia and at home.
